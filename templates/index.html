Why this exists

Compensation data is asymmetric: employers benchmark against market data, while candidates negotiate on anecdote. This tool gives a quick, model-based reference point.

Data imbalance

Companies hold internal and market-wide salary benchmarks that individuals rarely have access to.

Opaque signal

The factors that actually move a salary number — role, seniority, education — aren't obvious in isolation.

Negotiation anxiety

Without a reference point, candidates are more likely to anchor low and accept a sub-optimal offer.

Data pipeline

Raw survey data isn't usable as-is. Three preprocessing steps get it into model-ready shape.

Missing values

Rows missing a target (salary) or a critical feature are dropped; less critical gaps are imputed.

Feature encoding

Categorical fields — job title, education, gender — are label-encoded into a numeric form the model can read.

Scaling

Numerical features are standardized so no single feature dominates the model purely by having a larger range.

Where it's useful

Job applicant

A developer with five years of experience receives an offer. Before accepting, they check the model's estimate for their profile — a data point to bring into negotiation, not a final answer.

Hiring manager

Before setting a budget for a new role, a manager tests a few experience levels to sanity-check the range they're planning to offer against the model's estimate.

Limitations & ethics

Responsible use requires being upfront about what this model can and can't do.

This is a US-market model, shown in USD

Salary Data.csv — the only data this model was trained on — has no country or location field. It's a general US compensation dataset. Predictions are shown in USD, exactly as the model produces them; there's no currency conversion applied anywhere in this app, because converting a currency doesn't convert a labor market. If you need an India-specific (or any other market-specific) estimate, this model isn't calibrated for that — it would need to be retrained on data from that market.

No data retention

Inputs are used only to generate an instantaneous prediction. Nothing is stored or logged server-side.

Inherited bias

The model reflects patterns in its training data, including any historical pay disparities. Treat outputs as a reference, not ground truth.

Narrow scope

Trained on common corporate and tech roles. Not calibrated for niche professions, freelance work, or executive compensation.

Age dominates the model

Age drives ~58% of the prediction — more than job title and education combined. That's visible in every explanation below, and worth weighing when comparing this estimate to your own case.
